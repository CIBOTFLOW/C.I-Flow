AI COMMERCIAL PROBLEMS

The AI market is full of capability. The friction is commercialization.

My job is not to repeat that AI is transformative. It is to find the commercial obstacle behind the deal and design a path through it.

Differentiation collapse

What I see

Multiple vendors can now demo similar features. “Better AI” is a fragile differentiator when competitors can catch up quickly.

How I respond

I move the conversation to workflow ownership, integration, reliability, deployment speed, proprietary context, measurable outcome and the specific wedge where the buyer has pain.

Value-definition failure

What I see

The demo is impressive, but the CFO hears “productivity” and cannot connect it to a defendable business case.

How I respond

I quantify the value type the buyer actually funds: capacity, conversion, velocity, risk, management visibility, cost or strategic option value.

The pilot trap

What I see

A 90-day pilot becomes an experiment with no production decision, no stakeholder plan and no consequence if the metrics are met.

How I respond

I work backward from production before the pilot begins: success metrics, economic buyer, technical approval, implementation owner, decision date, commercial path and expansion criteria.

Implementation bottleneck

What I see

The buyer likes the product but security, data, IT bandwidth, CRM quality or another internal initiative becomes the real competitor.

How I respond

I surface implementation reality during discovery, reduce the first deployment to the minimum viable operational change, identify owners early and build a mutual path through dependencies.

Trust & AI risk

What I see

The more useful an AI system becomes—writing to systems, contacting customers, changing records—the more risk it creates for security and legal stakeholders.

How I respond

I bring controls, permissions, logging, human review, failure handling and security stakeholders into the value story instead of treating them as late-stage objections.

Buyer consensus

What I see

The business sponsor wants speed, IT wants control, finance wants proof, users fear disruption and procurement wants leverage.

How I respond

I treat the account as a decision system. The goal is not one champion—it is enough aligned evidence for the organization to say yes.

Renewal cliff

What I see

Initial AI enthusiasm can win a first contract. Renewal forces the buyer to ask what actually changed.

How I respond

I build outcome evidence during the contract, not at renewal: baseline, adoption, workflow completion, business consequence and unresolved opportunity.

Land-and-expand failure

What I see

A successful Sales or Support deployment stays isolated because nobody maps the adjacent workflows, stakeholders and budgets.

How I respond

I architect expansion around the next operating problem the first workflow reveals. Expansion should feel like solving the next logical problem, not selling more seats.

VALUE ARCHITECTURE

ROI is not one thing.

The useful question is not “Can I build an ROI calculator?” It is: which form of value matters enough to this specific buyer that they will fund the change?

Efficiency

Less human time required per task or workflow.

Capacity

The same team can manage more accounts, customers, cases or work.

Conversion

Better decisions or execution improve win, activation or resolution rates.

Velocity

Cycle time falls and value is realized sooner.

Risk

Fewer errors, missed accounts, compliance events or uncontrolled actions.

Option value

The organization can attempt a workflow or market motion that was previously uneconomic.

My rule

If AI is not the right answer, I would rather say that early. Trust is more valuable than forcing a bad implementation into a pipeline.
