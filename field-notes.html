How I think through hard commercial situations.

The interesting part of business development is not memorizing objections. It is deciding what the situation actually means and what action changes the probability of a deal.

01SituationWhat is actually happening?
02MeaningWhy does it matter commercially?
03RiskWhat could make the deal fail?
04ActionWhat changes the probability?
05EvidenceWhat would prove we are right?

Founder-led sales cannot scale.

My first 60 days: reconstruct the last 15 to 20 wins and losses, identify repeated use cases and buyer patterns, separate true product requirements from founder-specific customization, define the narrowest repeatable wedge, then build discovery and pilot criteria around that reality.

I would not rush to scale outbound before understanding what is actually repeatable.

“Microsoft will probably build this.”

I would not argue that Microsoft never will. I would ask what part of the value depends on us being structurally better: workflow depth, integration, domain context, deployment speed, service, governance, economics or a specialized operating problem the horizontal platform is unlikely to prioritize.

If none of those exist, the buyer may be right. We may have a positioning problem.

“Saved time is not saved money.”

The CFO may be right if the only claim is labor savings. So I would test the other value paths: can the same team handle more work, improve conversion, shorten cycles, reduce risk, avoid hiring, improve service levels or pursue a market motion that was previously uneconomic?

Time saved becomes valuable only when the organization can turn it into consequence.

A $200K prospect wants a free pilot.

I would not reject it automatically, but I would make the pilot a mutual decision instrument. Before starting: agreed problem, baseline, success metrics, economic buyer, technical owner, production decision date, implementation path and commercial terms if successful.

If they will not define what success changes, I would be cautious about giving away three months.

The champion wants to hide security.

I would not follow that path. Delaying a known veto point makes the forecast look better while making the deal weaker. I would work with the champion to introduce security with the right context: what actions the system can take, what controls exist, what data is touched, what can be constrained, and what a safe pilot looks like.
